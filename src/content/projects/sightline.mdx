_This case study documents Sightline's original photography-atlas experience. The live project is maintained separately and has since evolved._

## Photography with a sense of place

Sightline began as an independent interactive showcase for exploring my travel photography through geography. The original experience started with a fast, curated image archive, then let each supported city open into a three-dimensional scene where photographs could be read in relation to the streets, terrain, and skyline around them.

The project is deliberately separate from this portfolio's gallery. The portfolio remains the quiet editorial home for finished photographs; Sightline is the spatial experiment, with its own visual identity, release cadence, and public dataset.

## Moving between two dimensions and three

The central interaction is a reversible transition between an image-led city view and an immersive globe. Camera movement, selected photographs, URL state, and interface controls stay coordinated so the shift feels like another way of reading the same place rather than a jump into a separate tool.

The 2D experience remains useful on its own. Static routes, image-first browsing, and direct links keep the work accessible without requiring WebGL, while the enhanced viewer adds terrain, buildings, and guided viewpoints when the browser supports them.

## A reviewed public snapshot

Sightline publishes an explicit snapshot of the photography data rather than reaching into the portfolio at runtime. Public descriptions and date-only records travel with responsive images; source GPS, camera geometry, calibration evidence, and private archival material do not. Validation checks keep that boundary visible and make every release reproducible.

The showcase brings city archives and guided views together, with image-only fallbacks that preserve the photographs when an enhanced scene is unavailable.

## Built as a distinct instrument

Astro provides the static narrative and city routes, while CesiumJS and Google Photorealistic 3D Tiles power the optional spatial layer. The result is less a conventional map than an instrument for revisiting a place: select a photograph, enter its wider environment, and return to the archive without losing the thread.
